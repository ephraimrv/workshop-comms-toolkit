1. Reading an R command: the ~ symbol and friends

Most statistical commands in R are written as a formula using the tilde ~. Read the tilde as the words "depends on" or "by":

aov(Locomotive ~ group, data = d)
      ↑            ↑            ↑
   what you    the grouping   the table to
   measured    variable       find them in
 (the outcome) (the cause)

So Locomotive ~ group means "does Locomotive behaviour differ by group?" The thing you measured goes on the left; the thing that might explain it goes on the right.

In a formulaMeans
y ~ a"y depends on a" — one explanatory variable
y ~ a + b"y depends on a and b" — two variables, each on its own
y ~ a * ba, b, and their interaction — whether the effect of one depends on the other. Same as a + b + a:b.
y ~ a:bthe interaction only
data = d"find these columns in the table named d"
Why this matters The difference between ~ EMS + MO and ~ EMS * MO is the difference between "test each treatment" and "test each treatment and whether they combine." The * is how one command asks the richer question.

2. The p-value, and the significance stars

The p-value answers one question: if there were really no effect, how often would plain luck alone produce a result at least this large?

  • p below 0.05 — rarely by luck, so the effect is probably real.
  • p above 0.05 — could easily be luck, so you cannot claim an effect.

R prints little stars next to each p-value as a shorthand:

Signif. codes:  *** 0.001   ** 0.01   * 0.05   . 0.1   (blank) > 0.1
Three cautions
  • A p-value tells you whether there is an effect — not how big or how important it is. Always look at the actual means too.
  • 0.05 is a convention, not a law of nature. 0.049 and 0.051 are almost the same.
  • For the two assumption checks below (Shapiro, Bartlett), the meaning flips: a small p-value is a warning that the assumption has failed.

3. The tests, in plain language

FunctionWhat it does · how to read it
aov(y ~ group)
summary(...)		 ANOVA (Analysis of Variance). Compares the averages of several groups at once to ask "do any of them differ?" It works by weighing the variation between groups against the random variation within them. Read the Pr(>F) value — that is your p-value. This is the test GraphPad runs for group comparisons.
TukeyHSD(...) Tukey's Honest Significant Difference. An ANOVA only says "a difference exists somewhere." Tukey is the follow-up that says which specific pairs of groups differ, giving each pair its own p-value (p adj) already corrected for the fact that you are testing many pairs at once.
shapiro.test(...) Shapiro–Wilk test. Checks whether your data is roughly bell-shaped (normal) — one thing ANOVA assumes. A p-value below 0.05 means "not bell-shaped," i.e. a warning that plain ANOVA may not be trustworthy.
bartlett.test(...) Bartlett test. Checks whether the groups have a similar spread (variance) — the other thing ANOVA assumes. Again, p below 0.05 is a warning that the spreads differ too much for plain ANOVA.
kruskal.test(...) Kruskal–Wallis test. The back-up for ANOVA when Shapiro or Bartlett flag a problem. It asks the same "do the groups differ?" question but makes no bell-shape assumption — it works on the ranking of values instead of the values themselves. Read its p-value the same way.
chisq.test(...) Chi-squared test. A different family: for counts in categories, not measured numbers. Use it to ask "are these two categorical things related?" or "do these category counts differ from what I'd expect?" (e.g. observed vs. expected numbers of each behaviour type). Not interchangeable with ANOVA.
"Chi-squared" appears in two places — don't confuse them The chi-squared test above is for category counts. Separately, the Kruskal–Wallis output prints a line like chi-squared = 9.65 — there, "chi-squared" is just the statistic Kruskal–Wallis uses to get its p-value, not a chi-squared test. Same mathematical curve, different tests.

4. Reading an ANOVA results table

            Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value Pr(>F)
group        3   1016   338.7   2.868 0.0692 .
Residuals   16   1890   118.1
ColumnWhat it means
DfDegrees of freedom. For groups: number of groups − 1. For residuals: number of rows − number of groups.
Sum Sq / Mean SqHow much variation is explained by the groups vs. left over as noise. Mean Sq = Sum Sq ÷ Df.
F valueGroup signal ÷ leftover noise. A bigger F means the groups are more separated relative to the noise.
Pr(>F)The p-value. Here 0.069 — above 0.05, so this result alone does not establish a difference.

"Residuals" is simply the leftover part of each measurement after removing its group average — the noise the test compares against.

5. Which test do I use?

Your questionTest
Do the averages of 2 or more groups differ, and the data behaves (bell-shaped, similar spread)?aov() — ANOVA
Same question, but the data does not behave?kruskal.test()
ANOVA was significant — which groups differ?TukeyHSD()
Two grouping factors at once, and whether they interact?aov(y ~ a * b)
Are two categorical variables (counts) related?chisq.test()
Is my data bell-shaped / equally spread (before trusting ANOVA)?shapiro.test() · bartlett.test()

6. The supporting functions

FunctionWhat it does
read.csv("file.csv")Loads a CSV file into R as a table (a "data frame").
str(d)"Structure" — lists each column and the type R gave it. Run it first, always.
head(d)Shows the first few rows, to eyeball what loaded.
summary(...)On data: a quick numeric overview. On a fitted model: the results table.
tapply(d$y, d$group, mean)Applies a function (here mean) to a column, split by group — quick group averages.
factor(x, levels = ...)Tells R a column is a set of categories, and fixes their display order.
residuals(fit)The leftover noise from a fitted model — what the assumption checks are run on.
The workflow, in one line Inspect (str, head, means) → compare (aov) → follow up (TukeyHSD) → check it was valid (shapiro, bartlett) → switch test if needed (kruskal). Each step is one short command.
